How to read the top strip

Every chip in the strip shows three things: name, number, percent change. "S&P 500 7,623.68 −0.52%" means: the S&P 500 index last closed at 7,623.68 points, which is 0.52% lower than the previous day's close. Green is up, red is down. Always judge the percent, not the points: a 100-point move on the Dow and a 100-point move on the S&P are very different sizes.

Markets are only open weekdays, 6:30 AM to 1:00 PM Pacific. On weekends and holidays nothing is trading, so the numbers you see are the last close (on a Sunday, Friday's close).

S&P 500

Standard & Poor's 500. Not a company: an index, a single number that tracks 500 of the largest US companies lumped together, weighted by how big each company is. When a news anchor says "the market was up today," they almost always mean this. It is the broadest, most-watched snapshot of US big business, and the benchmark almost every fund measures itself against.

Dow 30

The Dow Jones Industrial Average, "the Dow." The oldest US index (1896). It tracks just 30 giant, long-established companies (Coca-Cola, Boeing, Home Depot, Goldman Sachs...) and averages them in an old-fashioned way that weights higher-priced stocks more. Because it is only 30 companies, it moves less than the S&P and is more a headline tradition than a precise market read.

Nasdaq

The Nasdaq Composite. An index of everything listed on the Nasdaq exchange, which is where tech lives: Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, Amazon and thousands more. When tech stocks are moving hard, the Nasdaq moves hardest. People read it as the "tech barometer."

10-Yr (Treasury yield)

The 10-Year Treasury yield. The US government borrows money by selling bonds; the yield is the interest rate it pays. The 10-year is the world's baseline "safe" rate. When it rises fast, borrowing gets more expensive for everyone (mortgages, car loans, companies), and stock valuations take pressure, especially young companies whose value sits far in the future. Around 5% is historically on the high side.

VIX

The "fear gauge." A number derived from options prices that measures how rocky investors expect stocks to be over the next month. Under 15 = calm. Around 20 = normal nerves. Over 30 = fear. Over 40 = panic. It is quoted like a percent (19.45 ≈ the market expects about 19% swings) but read as a mood.

Gold

The price of one troy ounce (31.1 grams) of gold. It tends to rise when people are scared and want something tangible, and fall when they would rather own stocks. Investors treat it as insurance, not growth.

Bitcoin

The price of one bitcoin, the largest cryptocurrency. It trades 24 hours a day, every day, and moves much more violently than stocks. Institutions now treat it as a high-risk asset that sometimes behaves like a tech stock.

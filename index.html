How to read the top strip

Each chip is name, rate, plain-English meaning. Hover over any chip for a one-liner. All the rates here are annual percentages: "4.98%" means 4.98% per year.

Treasuries: the government's IOUs

The US government borrows money by selling bonds of different lengths, from 3 months to 30 years. The yield is the interest rate each length pays. One rule to carry everywhere: when a yield rises, that bond's price fell, and vice versa. These yields are the baseline for nearly everything else: mortgages, car loans, corporate borrowing, even how analysts value stocks.

3M T-bill

A 3-month government IOU. It follows what the Federal Reserve is doing with short-term rates right now, and it is the closest thing to a guaranteed, risk-free return in the world. When cash sits in a money-market fund, it earns roughly this.

2Y, 10Y, 30Y

The same government borrowing at 2, 10 and 30 years. Shorter rates track what the Fed will do next; longer rates track expectations for growth and inflation years out. The 10Y is the one everything else is measured against. When the 2Y sits above the 10Y (an "inverted curve"), markets are usually bracing for a slowdown.

10Y TIPS

A 10-year Treasury whose payments rise with inflation (TIPS = Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities). Its yield is "real": inflation is already subtracted, so it shows the guaranteed return after inflation eats its share.

10Y breakeven

Normal 10Y yield minus the TIPS yield. That gap is the inflation rate bond markets expect over the next 10 years. A breakeven of 2.34% means the market prices in about 2.3% inflation a year, close to the Fed's target.

Savings FDIC / 12-mo CD FDIC

What ordinary insured US banks pay: the national average on a regular savings account, and on a 12-month certificate of deposit. Compare them with the T-bill to see whether the bank or the government pays you better for parking cash.

Unemployment

The share of the labor force out of work, from the Bureau of Labor Statistics. It is the labor market's temperature: low is good for workers, but too low can push the Fed to keep rates high.
